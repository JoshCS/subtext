# Subtext — Plan v2

*November 2026. Supersedes the v1 plan. Evidence for the claims below is in [RESEARCH.md](RESEARCH.md) (9,400 words, ~300 sources); the design detail is in [UX.md](UX.md), [CALIBRATION.md](CALIBRATION.md), [DATA-SOURCES.md](DATA-SOURCES.md), [LICENSING.md](LICENSING.md) and [THREAT-MODEL.md](THREAT-MODEL.md).*

## 1. What Subtext is

Subtext reads the page you are on and shows you its subtext: **what it is doing to you** (dark patterns, urgency, social proof, rhetoric, emotional and interpersonal manipulation, misleading charts), **who is behind it** (ownership, funding, partisan lean by third-party raters) and **what they get if you comply** (business model, data sale and brokerage, affiliate commissions, lead capture, the clauses in the privacy policy and terms). Every flag is a short warning label, a one-line reason, and on click the full explanation of the psychology behind it and a one-minute counter-move.

Three principles decide everything else:

1. **Effect, not intent.** Regulators have converged on effect-based standards (the California CPPA's "effect, not intent", Australia's "unreasonably manipulates or distorts"), which is exactly the standard a detector can meet: we measure asymmetry, timing, step counts and geometry; we never claim to know motive. Labels name the sign, why-lines state mechanism or evidence, and the word "fake" appears only where behaviour proved it (a timer that restarted on reload).
2. **Evidence first, symmetric always.** No finding without a quote, an element, a measurement or a sourced dataset row. Rhetoric and emotion rules are written for moves, not sides, and the evaluation plan checks that they fire at comparable rates on left- and right-leaning pages, because the research shows text classifiers (LLMs included) have an asymmetric false-positive rate.
3. **Local first, one lens.** Scanning runs in the browser and sends nothing anywhere by default. The AI layer is opt-in with the user's own key. Everything that today lives in five different tools (news raters, tracker blockers, dark-pattern catalogues, terms graders, funding databases) appears in one place with one shape.

## 2. What the research changed

Seven research tracks (dark patterns and regulation; attention and AI-interface manipulation; rhetoric and propaganda NLP; ownership, incentives and privacy policies; trackers, charts, reviews and evasion; warning UX, licensing and governance; scoring, evaluation and LLM APIs) produced the report in RESEARCH.md. The conclusions that reshape the plan:

- **The gap is real.** No maintained, open, consumer-grade detector reads the live page in any of Subtext's domains: Dapde's Pattern Highlighter was archived in July 2024, Fakespot closed on 1 July 2025, Logically entered administration in July 2025, and Mozilla retired *Privacy Not Included* in 2026. Published detectors classify screenshots or text, not the DOM, and the literature contains no DOM-level detector for infinite scroll, autoplay, notification prompts or streak widgets.
- **Structure beats text.** The best multimodal dark-pattern classifier scores micro-F1 0.50 on website screenshots (0.35 macro); the best SemEval-2023 persuasion-technique system reaches 37.6% micro-F1 over 23 techniques; prompted frontier models trail fine-tuned encoders badly on propaganda (macro-F1 0.16 vs 0.67) and systematically over-flag. The signals only an extension has (computed-style prominence asymmetry, DOM diffs over time, reset timers, step counts, consent-manager geometry, request graphs, SVG geometry) are both more accurate and, per the ad-blocking literature, harder to evade. Text rules stay as a supporting layer with calibrated thresholds.
- **The taxonomy should be a crosswalk, not an invention.** Anchor to Gray et al.'s CHI 2024 ontology (64 types: detect at the low level, explain at meso/high), the 11 attention-capture damaging patterns, SemEval's 23 persuasion techniques, MAFALDA's 22 fallacies, MentalManip's 11 manipulation techniques, DarkBench's 6 and CDT's 37 AI-chatbot patterns, Misviz's 12 chart misleaders and Tracker Radar's 22 tracker categories. Each Subtext finding will carry its crosswalk ids and, where one exists, its enforcement anchor.
- **Label in regulators' vocabulary.** The FTC's $2.5bn Amazon Prime settlement (asymmetric prominence, "Iliad" cancellation), the EU's first DSA Article 25 fine (€120m against X for a misleading verification badge), CNIL's €325m cookie-banner fine, the UK's first drip-pricing penalty (£4.2m), the DSA addictive-design findings against TikTok and Meta (infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications), New York's SAFE for Kids 20%-of-time rule, the FTC fake-reviews rule and the UK's CMA208 all name patterns Subtext flags. The Digital Fairness Act proposal is due the week this plan was written. A finding that can cite an enforcement action is a different product from one that cites a blog post.
- **Licences constrain the data.** Tracker Radar, Disconnect, Ghostery's TrackerDB, MentalManip, the NRC lexicons and the Yale outrage classifier are non-commercial-licensed and cannot be bundled into an AGPL or MIT extension; WhoTracks.me statistics (CC BY), FP-Inspector's list (MIT), AidUI/ContextDP (MIT), UIGuard (Apache-2.0), Misviz data (CC BY-SA) and the yamanalab e-commerce text set (Apache-2.0) can.
- **Warnings have a literature.** Fact-check labels work (meta-analysis, 21 experiments), but generic warnings spill over onto honest content and unlabelled items gain an "implied truth"; warnings habituate unless their form varies; opinionated visual design raised adherence far more than wording; the glance→hover→click stack is the proven convention. This is why Subtext's default is a quiet pill, a strict chip budget, specific labels, and markers that are off until asked for.
- **Open rules are the right call.** SpamAssassin's public-rules history and the ad-blocking arms race show that publishing rules did not lose the war; what wins is behavioural and structural measurement plus update cadence. Licence enforcement against AI-laundered copies is weak (the Ninth Circuit's September 2026 Copilot ruling removed the main output-side lever), so the moat is the living rule base, the datasets, the benchmark and the trademark, not the copyright.

## 4. The incentives layer

"What do they get from you?" turns out to be the most clarifying question a reader can ask of any page, and the research found almost no product that answers it. Subtext v0.2 answers it four ways:

1. **Business model, inferred** from the page itself: ad-tech vendors, profiling vendors, affiliate links, subscription and checkout signals, lead-capture forms, donation asks, sponsored labels. Shown as an informational chip so every other finding is read in its light.
2. **Data signals:** a "Do Not Sell or Share" link is, under California law, a statement that the site sells or shares personal data unless you opt out; data-broker and identity-graph vendors on the page mean your visit can be joined to a purchasable profile; "free to use" plus heavy profiling means you are the product.
3. **The policy, read for you:** 21 clause types scanned in the linked privacy policy and terms, with the sentence quoted and a link to it: sale of data, sharing for partners' marketing, brokers, profile building, precise location, biometrics, cross-device, indefinite retention, forced arbitration, unilateral changes, children's data, automated decisions, email tracking, hard opt-outs, data sold with the company; and the positives: no-sale statements, GPC honoured, deletion rights. Negations are handled ("we do not sell" becomes a positive signal).
4. **Disclosure gaps:** affiliate links without a disclosure, sponsored labels that are tiny or low-contrast, organisation-style names with no funding page, missing mastheads.

Roadmap: ToS;DR's point-level data (its grading algorithm is public: good − bad − 3×blocker), a business-model classifier following the 2026 decision-tree paper (ad-supported / paywall / transactional / mixed), cross-referencing vendors against the California data-broker registry (600+ brokers, with deletion obligations from August 2026), ads.txt and sellers.json for ad-supply chains, and disclosure-placement checks (is the affiliate disclosure above the first affiliate link?).

## 5. Communicating insights

The full specification is UX.md. The decisions:

- **Four tiers.** Tier 0: the toolbar badge and a pill reading `Subtext F · 3 serious` (no raw counts: counts train people to stop looking). Tier 1: warning-sign labels, 1–3 words, category icon, severity colour, at most five on the glance strip and at most two per category. Tier 2: one line under 12 words, mechanism or evidence, no judgement. Tier 3: technique, explanation, "why it works on you" from the bias glossary, evidence with click-to-locate, a one-minute counter-move, source links.
- **Copy rules** (eight of them in UX.md): labels are noun phrases naming the sign; "fake" only with behavioural proof; why-lines state mechanism or evidence and ban words like fraud, propaganda, scam; counter-moves start with a verb. All copy lives in `rules/labels.json` and is linted (no why-line over 12 words, no label over three).
- **Nothing is drawn on the page.** No outlines or boxes: hovering a flagged element shows a tooltip with the label and why; the breakdown lives in a resizable panel docked to the right, opened from the pill or the popup. Everything the extension shows lives in one closed shadow root (4 Nov 2026), so sites cannot restyle it and nothing is written onto site elements for display. Small dot markers exist behind a toggle, off by default.
- **Fatigue budgets:** five chips, informational findings collapsed, one-click mutes per site and per category, a "this is wrong" button, and polymorphic phrasing (several why-lines per finding, rotated) because fixed warnings habituate.
- **Evaluation:** a comprehension test (n = 40: can users explain a chip?), a behaviour test (200 per arm pilot), and a two-week fatigue diary (n = 20), modelled on Felt et al.'s browser-warning work and the Dark Pita study.

## 6. Scoring and calibration

A page's grade is `100 × (1 − e^(−w/20))` over per-category-capped weights (severity × confidence × √count), with floors so that one proven severe finding from behaviour, structure, network, policy text or the dataset is at least a C. Lexical matches cannot trigger floors, because text can be planted. Synthetic calibration across six intensity levels holds (0 → 18 → 21 → 58 → 71 → 91); the control page is 0; the worst cases are 100. The real calibration protocol (200-page stratified corpus, two annotators, κ targets, two AI judges, per-rule precision targets of 0.85–0.9, symmetry check within 10 points, fatigue diary) is in CALIBRATION.md. Comparable systems (NewsGuard's weighted binary criteria, ToS;DR's blocker rule, Lighthouse's weights) all publish the per-criterion breakdown and keep the verdict label decoupled from the raw number; so does Subtext.

## 7. The AI layer

Both providers are supported through one module with one prompt and one strict JSON schema (closed objects, every property required, string enums only, no numeric constraints, no recursion, so it validates on both `output_config.format` and `text.format`). Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-5.5 are the defaults; the cheap tiers (Haiku 5.5, GPT-5.4 nano) analyse a typical page for well under a tenth of a cent, the large tiers for roughly three to four cents; a per-day page cap belongs in the UI. The model is told to find what regex cannot (rhetorical structure, omitted context, headline–body contradictions, interpersonal tactics, the business model and what the site gains) and never to repeat the heuristic layer. Known failure modes from the research drive the next steps: a runtime verifier that drops any finding whose quote is not in the visible text, calibrated confidence, two providers used as independent judges in evaluation, and the prompt tuned against over-flagging (reasoning models especially) and against treating profanity or quoted speech as manipulation. Hidden page text never reaches the model: extraction uses the visible-text index.

## 8. Data

DATA-SOURCES.md is current with these updates from the research: OpenSecrets closed new API registrations in April 2025 (bulk data remains, account-gated); LittleSis has a documented API with batch caps; EurOMo published an EU media-owners database in September 2025; MEDIAREF (July 2026) released a public knowledge store for automated media background checks; Media Bias/Fact Check offers a limited API tier; ToS;DR's current public API is v3; the California data-broker registry lists 600+ brokers. The v0.5 pipeline generates outlets from Wikidata + LittleSis + EurOMo + 990 data with two sources per row, a rater-and-date on every lean label, a takedown/correction process, and a seven-day correction SLA. The tracker list stays in-house (Tracker Radar's CC BY-NC-SA cannot be bundled), enriched from WhoTracks.me (CC BY) and FP-Inspector (MIT).

## 9. Licensing and governance

Recommendation (detail and precedents in LICENSING.md): **AGPL-3.0-or-later** for code; **CC BY-SA 4.0** for rules, labels, taxonomy, datasets, test corpus and docs; a registered **"Subtext" trademark** with a policy requiring forks to rename; **DCO** rather than a CLA (so no one, including us, can relicense unilaterally); a fiscal host (Open Collective or Software Freedom Conservancy) once there is money. AGPL is OSI-approved, store-distributable, and was the licence Elastic and Redis returned to after source-available detours triggered foundation forks. It does not stop private use or honest renamed forks; combined with the trademark and attribution it stops *silent* ones, which is the stated goal. The canonical licence text must be copied from gnu.org, not reproduced.

## 10. Copying, evasion and misuse

THREAT-MODEL.md ranks the layers by evasion cost and finds what the ad-blocking literature found: lexical rules are cheap to evade or to trigger falsely; behavioural, structural and network measurements are not, and evading them usually removes the harm (deleting the fake countdown, adding a real reject button). Policy: publish mechanisms and rules; keep a small private hold-out set for evaluation only; sign rule and data feeds for fast updates; no "make my page pass" service and no "Subtext Verified" badge (it would itself be a trust-badge pattern); an evasion and false-positive reporting channel (SECURITY.md); a usage clause in the trademark policy. The highest-priority finding was about the extension itself: its injected pill, classes, data attributes and web-accessible rule files make it fingerprintable, and a detected extension can be served a clean page. The v0.3 backlog moves the overlay into a shadow root with randomised identifiers, replaces data attributes with internal maps, drops web-accessible resources, and fetches policies only on demand.

## 11. Roadmap

**v0.3 — hardening (weeks).** The seven small threat-model items (shadow-root overlay, no data attributes, no web-accessible resources, minCount/scope on severity-2+ lexical rules, visible-text extraction with runtime quote verification, on-demand same-domain policy fetch, SECURITY.md); the UX gap list (keyboard path, dialog role, polymorphic why-lines, "this is wrong"); label lint in the validator; taxonomy crosswalk ids and enforcement anchors on findings; Firefox build.

**v0.5 — corpus and pipeline (1–2 months).** The 200-page labelled corpus and the first per-rule precision report; the two-provider AI evaluation; the verifier pass; the outlets pipeline; ToS;DR points and the data-broker registry; evasion findings (text-as-image, hidden disclosures, anti-extension scripts); step counting across flows; price/stock memory; Chrome side panel; signed feeds; private hold-out set.

**v1.0 — release (3–4 months).** Chrome Web Store, Edge, Safari App Store wrapper, Firefox AMO; published benchmark and precision per rule; trademark registered; licence files in place; fiscal host; dataset governance live.

**Later.** Shared, versioned rule and data feeds with crowd reports; platform adapters for feeds and AI chat interfaces (DarkBench/CDT patterns need transcript access); personal insights; an API/MCP server so other tools can ask what a page is doing.

## 12. Risks

Unchanged from v1 in kind, sharper in detail: defamation (sourced rows, correction SLA, no motive language); false positives and fatigue (precision targets, budgets, symmetric corpus); politicisation (symmetry check in CI); privacy of the extension itself (local by default, opt-in AI and ToS;DR, same-domain policy fetch, spend-limited keys); fingerprinting and clean-page serving (shadow root, randomised ids); data-licence contamination (non-commercial sources excluded); store review of host permissions (the product is the justification); single-source facts in the research (flagged inline in RESEARCH.md).
