# Which rules are reliable, and why

The owner's rule, 5 November 2026: only rules that have proved reliable may flag. A third of flags being wrong will not work.

Each rule was tested on what it actually fired on across 126 recorded real pages. Independent AI judges rated each firing as useful, true but ordinary, or wrong, read the rule's code, and named the cause. **No person has checked these verdicts.** The recorded pages are not in the public repository, so this report cannot be regenerated from a clone.

- **Flag:** shown to readers. The rule has at least 5 judged firings, 15% or fewer wrong and 50% or more useful; or it is a vendor fact measured from the page, a direct proof that shows on its first firing (a countdown that resets on reload), or a listing by a named third party.
- **Quiet note:** true but ordinary. It appears under "Also noticed" and never as a warning.
- **Held:** still runs and is recorded, but readers do not see it unless they turn on "Show unproven checks". Every new rule starts here.

Two general rules sit on top. A flag with no evidence a reader can check is demoted to a note. A claim that something is absent, seen from one page, is a note at most, because footers load late and terms live elsewhere.

**One exception, 6 November 2026 (owner: "we're about surfacing the subtext on the page").** A finding that only states plain facts about a shop page (`checkedFacts` in `rules/reliability.json`: no social profile, no phone, no company name and the like, each with how often listed and honest shops show it) shows at its own weight, absences included, because the truth of each fact is measured: `eval/fact-check.py` has a second reader find each missing thing on the page, and code confirms the quote. On 121 archived shop pages about 3% of stated absences were shown false, probably under 5% once the second reader's misses are allowed for (CALIBRATION section 10). The wording says "on this page" and never calls the shop dishonest. Only the rare combination (no real social profile, no phone, no company name) reaches the top line.

What the analysis found, by family:
- **How text argues:** word matching cannot do this. Of 41 firings 2 were right and 31 wrong, and rules naming a fallacy never fired on 126 pages because writers do not announce the move. The family is held, and Deep analyze reads argument instead.
- **Sale language:** of what shipped, 19% was wrong but 60% was ordinary. The "does this page sell" gate does the real work. Behaviour and structure prove more than wording does.
- **Privacy and terms clauses:** the strongest family, 52% useful and 15% wrong. Three flaws in how sentences were split and assigned caused most misfires.
- **Measured checks:** 42% useful and 24% wrong, clustered in a few rules with one code flaw each. Buy-side advertising pixels had been read as ad slots.

Regenerate this file with `python3 eval/reliability-report.py <folder>`. The per-rule data is in `data/reliability-ledger.json`. Promote a rule only with evidence from pages it was not tuned on.
