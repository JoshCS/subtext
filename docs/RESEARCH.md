# Detect Structure, Publish Rules, License Copyleft

As of November 2026 the manipulation-detection field has an enforcement record, a settled set of taxonomies and a graveyard of consumer tools, but no maintained open-source detector that reads the live page — and that gap is exactly where Subtext should build. Regulators now fine the same patterns Subtext flags (the FTC's **$2.5bn Amazon Prime settlement**, the EU's first DSA deceptive-design fine of **€120m against X**, CNIL's **€325m** cookie-banner fine against Google, the UK's first drip-pricing penalty), and the EU Digital Fairness Act proposal is due **11 November 2026**, so the project can label findings in regulators' own vocabulary and under their "effect, not intent" standard. Published detection research is weakest precisely on Subtext's terrain: the best multimodal classifier reaches **micro-F1 0.50 on website screenshots**, the best SemEval-2023 persuasion-technique system reached **37.6% micro-F1** on English, and prompted frontier LLMs score **0.16 macro-F1 versus 0.67 for a fine-tuned RoBERTa** on span-level propaganda while systematically over-flagging. The extension's unique assets are structural and temporal — computed-style asymmetry, DOM diffs that catch resetting countdowns, click-path step counts, CMP rule sets, tracker ownership graphs — which no screenshot or text model sees and which adversaries find hardest to disguise. Subtext should reuse the Gray et al. ontology, the SemEval-2023 23-technique hierarchy, CDT's 37 chatbot patterns, DuckDuckGo autoconsent, ToS;DR, LittleSis and WhoTracks.me; build DOM-level attention and flow detectors, an SVG chart linter and a labelled, kappa-reported corpus; and avoid image-based chart detection, fine-grained LLM span tagging, NC-licensed lexicons and the dozen unlicensed hackathon repos. Warnings should name the specific technique, vary their appearance to resist habituation, and show an explicit "nothing detected" state; scores should be weighted binary criteria with a blocker cap, an N/A state and a grade decoupled from the verdict label. For governance, the 2023–2025 relicensing episodes argue for **AGPLv3 code plus a foundation-held trademark policy and DCO**, not a source-available licence, and because the Ninth Circuit foreclosed the main DMCA lever against AI-generated code copies in September 2026, the real moat is update cadence, signed releases and community. On dual use, the spam-filter and ad-block literatures say publish the rules: evasion happens regardless, review improves rules, and behavioural signals outlast lexical ones.

## Enforcement has caught up with the taxonomies: $2.5bn, €120m and a Digital Fairness Act due 11 November

The field's centre of gravity moved from guidance to penalties between 2023 and 2026, which changes what a detector can claim. The FTC's Amazon Prime settlement of **25 September 2025** combined a **$1bn civil penalty**, the largest ever for an FTC rule violation, with **$1.5bn in refunds to roughly 35 million consumers**, and rested on a checkout that made declining enrolment "less prominent" than enrolling and a cancellation flow staff nicknamed "Iliad" ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/409399/amazon-settles-dark-patterns-charges-for-25-bi.html); [Jurist](https://www.jurist.org/news/?p=294008)). It followed Epic Games' **$245m** order for Fortnite purchase dark patterns ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/80648)), a complaint alleging Uber One took "a couple of clicks" to join and **up to 32 actions** to leave ([Gigazine](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20250422-ftc-sues-uber-deceptive-subscriptions/)), and Match Group's **$14m** settlement for lacking a simple cancellation method ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/408129/match-group-settles-with-ftc-for-14m-promises-si.html)). The Eighth Circuit vacated the Click-to-Cancel rule on **8 July 2025** solely because the FTC skipped the preliminary regulatory analysis required by FTC Act §22 ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/consumer-trading-unfair-trading/1652134/eighth-circuit-cancels-ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule)); ROSCA enforcement continued and the agency reopened negative-option rulemaking with an ANPRM in March 2026 ([Kelley Drye](https://s3.amazonaws.com/cdn.kelleydrye.com/content/uploads/pdf-snapshots/ftc-relaunches-negative-option-rulemaking-with-new-questions-new-branding-20260312151119.pdf)). California's CPPA wrote five consent-design principles into 11 CCR §7004 — symmetry of choice, no double negatives, no guilt or bundled consent — and declared that "any agreement obtained through the use of dark patterns shall not constitute consumer consent" ([Cornell LII](https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/11-CCR-7004)); its first order fined Honda **$632,500** for a banner that made opting out harder than opting in ([Transcend](https://transcend.io/blog/honda-fine-ccpa-violation)). Its 2024 enforcement advisory states the standard that matters most for an automated tool: **effect, not intent, governs**, and an opt-in offering only "yes" and "ask me later" is the canonical asymmetry ([CPPA](https://www.cppa.ca.gov/pdf/enfadvisory202402.pdf)).

Europe supplied the first deceptive-design fine under the Digital Services Act and the first addictive-design findings. The Commission fined X **€120m on 5 December 2025**, holding that the paid blue checkmark "misrepresents verification" in breach of Article 25(1) ([Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-fines-x-eu120-million-under-digital-services-act)) — making a misleading trust signal a legally recognised pattern. Preliminary findings in October 2025 said Meta and TikTok "appear to use dark patterns" in their illegal-content reporting flows ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/24/ec-finds-meta-and-tiktok-breached-transparency-rules-under-dsa/?rand=324)); the **6 February 2026** TikTok findings and **10 July 2026** Meta findings named infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and engagement-driven recommenders as addictive design, demanded autoplay and infinite scroll off by default, and did so under the Article 34–35 risk-assessment duties because "addictive design" appears nowhere in the DSA text ([Commission on TikTok](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-preliminarily-finds-tiktoks-addictive-design-breach-digital-services-act); [Commission IP/26/1579](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_1579); [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/tiktok-dsa/)). The Digital Fairness Act proposal is listed for **11 November 2026** ([digitalfairnessact.com](https://digitalfairnessact.com/historic-timeline); [EPRS](https://eprs.europarl.europa.eu/contents/publications/EPRS/2026/10/EPRS_BRI(2026)791529.html)), with an EU-wide definition, a non-exhaustive blacklist, a general prohibition and a "fairness by design" duty all under discussion, partly because the 2024 Fitness Check found subscription traps "not explicitly prohibited" under existing law ([Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/10/eu-digital-fairness-act-dark-patterns-and-addictive-designs)). The AI Act's Article 5 ban on subliminal, manipulative or deceptive techniques has applied since **2 February 2025** with penalties since 2 August 2025, and the Commission's guidelines of 4 February 2025 say intent to harm is sufficient but not necessary and that the system itself, not only its designer, can be the manipulator ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/insights/2025/04/eu-commission-publishes-guidelines-on-the-prohibited-ai-practices-under-the-ai-act); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/ai-outlook/2025/european-commission-publishes-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices)). CNIL fined Google **€325m** and Shein **€150m** in September 2025 for banners that "nudged users toward accepting cookies through manipulative design" ([Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/en/insights/2025/09/19/when-the-cookie-crumbles-lessons-from-the-cnil-102l64c)).

Reviews, pricing and disclosure now have statutory definitions a detector can quote. In the UK, drip pricing and fake reviews became automatically unfair practices enforceable directly by the CMA from April 2025, with fines up to 10% of global turnover; the first penalty was **£4.2m against AA driving schools** for drip pricing in April 2026, three further drip-pricing probes opened in August 2026, and five fake-review investigations are running ([Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/uk/consumer-law-reform-understanding-subscription-contracts,-fake-reviews-and-drip-pricing); [HSF Kramer](https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/crt/2026-07/cma-launches-three-further-drip-pricing-investigations-under-the-dmcc-act-consumer-protection-regime)). CMA208 guidance defines the banned practice as fake reviews and "concealed incentivised reviews" ([gov.uk CMA208](https://gov.uk/government/publications/fake-reviews-cma208)). The FTC's Consumer Reviews rule (16 CFR Part 465) took effect **21 October 2024**, covering AI-generated reviews, bought reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled review sites and fake social-media indicators, with civil penalties inflation-adjusted to **$53,088 per violation** ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-08-22/html/2024-18519.htm); [etcentric](https://www.etcentric.org/ftc-rule-prohibiting-fake-and-paid-reviews-goes-into-effect/)). India's CCPA enumerated **13 specified dark patterns** in November 2023 — false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing, disguised advertisement, nagging, trick question, SaaS billing, rogue malwares ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/buzz/govt-issues-list-of-13-dark-patterns-plaguing-ecommerce-websites)) — and in June 2025 ordered 50-plus platforms to self-audit within three months ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/industry/news/ccpa-dark-patterns-advisory-online-platforms-125060800474_1.html)). Korea's amended E-Commerce Act regulated six pattern types from **14 February 2025**, including hidden renewals, drip pricing and nagging ([MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2295614/south-korea-amends-e-commerce-decree-to-regulate-online-dark-patterns)); Canada's Competition Tribunal fined Cineplex **$38.9m** for a mandatory booking fee ([Competition Bureau](https://www.canada.ca/en/competition-bureau/news/2024/09/competition-bureau-wins-deceptive-marketing-case-against-cineplex.html)); Australia's exposure draft would ban conduct that "unreasonably manipulates" or "unreasonably distorts" consumer decisions from 1 July 2027 ([Bird & Bird](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/australia/the-end-of-fine-print-proposed-reform-to-australia's-unfair-trading-practices)). The scale of the problem is stable across a decade of measurement: ICPEN's 2024 sweep found **75.7% of 642 subscription sites** used at least one possible dark pattern and **81% of auto-renewing services removed the ability to disable auto-renewal at purchase**, while GPEN found 97% of about 1,010 sites and apps used a privacy dark pattern ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/85835)). Cookie compliance remains the exception: around 67% of sites show a banner but only about **15% meet minimum legal requirements**, mostly for want of a reject option ([arXiv 2503.19655](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19655)), and only **12% of sites** with a US Privacy String honoured Global Privacy Control in a 2023 audit ([PoPETs 2023](https://petsymposium.org/popets/2023/popets-2023-0052.php)). Disclosure is worse: over **95% of sponsored influencer posts on Twitter were undisclosed** and the share barely moved with enforcement ([INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/references/10.1287/mksc.2024.0838)), and only **45.52% of affiliate-linked YouTube videos** carried any disclosure ([Hello Partner](https://hellopartner.com/youtube-affiliate-marketing-booms-as-disclosure-standards-continue-to-lag-new-research-finds/)).

For attention engineering and AI interfaces, US states supplied measurable definitions. The Ninth Circuit on **9 September 2025** upheld California SB 976's "addictive feed" and default-privacy provisions while enjoining its like-count restriction as protected expression ([EPIC](https://epic.org/ninth-circuit-rejects-netchoices-attack-on-californias-addictive-feed-regulation/)) — the one mechanic courts have so far shielded is displayed information, not delivery mechanics. New York's SAFE for Kids rules, final on 28 July 2026 and effective 25 January 2027, call a platform addictive when **at least 20% of monthly-active-user time** goes to addictive feeds over six months ([NY AG](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-and-governor-hochul-release-final-safe-kids-act-rules)). California SB 243 on companion chatbots took effect 1 January 2026 with AI-disclosure and self-harm protocols ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law)), and the FTC opened a 6(b) study of companion chatbots' engagement monetisation in September 2025 ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-launches-inquiry-ai-chatbots-acting-companions)). The harm evidence behind all this is strongest for quiet patterns: in Luguri and Strahilevitz's representative US experiment, acceptance of a dubious identity-protection plan rose from about **11% (control) to 26% (mild dark patterns) to 42% (aggressive)**, and mild patterns provoked no backlash while aggressive ones did ([OUP JLA](https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/13/1/43/6180579)). UK impact assessments put unwanted subscriptions at **£1.6bn a year** ([gov.uk](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/64464e6f529eda00123b02c4/annex_2-subscription_traps_impact_assessment.pdf)). Awareness does not protect: 406 survey respondents knew manipulative design shaped their behaviour yet could not resist it ([arXiv 2104.12653](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12653v1)), and in a 589-user experiment most people missed dark patterns unless primed ([Di Geronimo et al.](https://fpalomba.github.io/pdf/Conferencs/C45.pdf)).

The practical output of this record is a legally grounded core of patterns where every flag can cite an action or statute, which Subtext should mark in its taxonomy and surface in the UI.

Two features of this record shape the whole design. First, jurisdictions converge on effect-based standards — the CPPA's "effect, not intent", Australia's "unreasonably manipulates or distorts" — which legitimises a detector that measures interface effects such as asymmetry, step counts and timing rather than inferring motive. Second, regulators measure through platform disclosure and risk assessments rather than through the DOM ([Commission on Meta minors](https://cyprus.representation.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-preliminarily-finds-meta-breach-digital-services-act-failing-prevent-minors-under-13-2026-04-29_en)), so an independent, page-level observer that outputs the same vocabulary is complementary to, not duplicative of, enforcement.

## Four taxonomies cover the label space: Gray's 64, SemEval's 23, ACDP's 11 and CDT's 37

Subtext's `TAXONOMY.md` should be a crosswalk, not an invention. For deceptive design the anchor is Gray, Santos, Bielova and Mildner's CHI 2024 ontology, which harmonises ten regulatory and academic taxonomies into **64 types across six high-level, 25 meso-level and 35 low-level patterns**, and whose authors explicitly describe the low-level layer as "the most context-specific" and "the most promising to detect" while reserving meso and high levels for policy ([arXiv 2309.09640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09640); [CHI 2024](https://programs.sigchi.org/chi/2024/program/content/147221)). That split is the right architecture: detect at the low level, explain and map to regulation at the meso and high levels. Larger syntheses exist — Li and Wang's **68-type** taxonomy, whose survey found existing detectors cover only about **45.5%** of known types ([arXiv 2412.09147](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.09147v1)); Isola and Esposito's 42 types mapped to the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive ([deceptive.design](https://deceptive.design/articles/a-systematic-literature-review-on-dark-patterns-for-the-legal-community-definitional-clarity-and-a-legal-classification-based-on-the-unfair-commercial-practices-directive)); and Shi et al.'s detection-oriented **24 subcategories** with a 6,725-image dataset ([arXiv 2501.13351](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13351)). The CHI 2025 "Iliad Flow" paper adds the temporal dimension prior taxonomies lack, analysing patterns that unfold across multi-step flows ([arXiv 2309.09635](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.09635v2)). Regulators each keep shorter lists — EDPB's six categories (Overloading, Skipping, Stirring, Obstructing, Fickle, Left in the dark) ([activemind.legal](https://activemind.legal/guides/dark-patterns)), the CMA's **21 online-choice-architecture practices** in three groups ([CMA](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/624c27c68fa8f527710aaf58/Online_choice_architecture_discussion_paper.pdf)), India's 13, OECD's categories — and every one of them states the list is non-exhaustive, so Subtext should version its taxonomy and record provenance per label. The ontology's own count moved from 65 to 64 between preprint and publication, a reminder that even the canonical list is a moving target.

For attention engineering, Monge Roffarello, Lukoff and De Russis's CHI 2023 "attention-capture damaging patterns" typology of **11 types** — infinite scroll, neverending autoplay, pull-to-refresh, guilty-pleasure recommendations among them — distinguishes patterns that deceive from those that seduce with short-term reward, and is the vocabulary regulators have since adopted ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544548.3580729)). Participants in the 2025 "Purpose Mode" study, which toggled seven of these patterns on X, Facebook, LinkedIn and YouTube, also reported distractions the typology omits, such as cluttered layouts and short-form video ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3711841)). The evidence hierarchy matters for confidence language: loot-box spending correlates with problem-gambling symptoms at **r = 0.26 to 0.37** across 15 studies ([meta-analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/351107695_Meta-analysis_of_the_relationship_between_problem_gambling_excessive_gaming_and_loot_box_spending)); replacing autoplay with explicit play "significantly increases perceived agency and reduces time distortion" in a within-subjects lab study, with weaker effects for pagination over infinite scroll ([IRIS Polito](https://iris.polito.it/retrieve/handle/11583/3008098/981734)); and no large randomised trial isolating any single mechanic's wellbeing effect exists, which is why the Commission acts under risk-assessment duties rather than proven harm.

For rhetoric, the Da San Martino, Piskorski and Nakov lineage is the standard. SemEval-2023 Task 3 used "a pool of 23 techniques" in a two-tier hierarchy across nine languages ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2023.semeval-1.317.pdf)); the labels, verbatim from the winning team's file, run from Appeal_to_Authority and Appeal_to_Fear-Prejudice through Straw_Man, Red_Herring, Whataboutism, Loaded_Language, Exaggeration-Minimisation and Name_Calling-Labeling to Questioning_the_Reputation ([KInIT](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kinit-sk/semeval2023-task3-persuasion-techniques/main/techniques_subtask3.txt)). SemEval-2024 Task 4 reused 22 of them as a directed acyclic graph and awarded partial credit when a system predicted an ancestor node ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.semeval-1.275)) — the right template for hedged labels when a fine technique is uncertain. For fallacies, MAFALDA unifies LOGIC, Argotario and ElecDeb60to20 into one span-level taxonomy with **22 leaf labels**, uses a disjunctive scheme that allows several correct labels because of "the inherent subjectivity of fallacy annotation", and releases code and data under CC BY-SA ([arXiv 2311.09761](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.09761); [gold data](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ChadiHelwe/MAFALDA/main/datasets/gold_standard_dataset.jsonl)). The two inventories overlap heavily — straw man, red herring, false dilemma, causal oversimplification, appeals to fear, authority and popularity, guilt by association, ad hominem — so one combined label space of roughly 25 to 30 items covers both literatures. Media bias is consolidated in MBIB (22 datasets across eight tasks, GPL-3.0) with the expert-labelled BABE corpus at its core ([MBIB](https://github.com/Media-Bias-Group/MBIB); [BABE](https://github.com/Media-Bias-Group/Neural-Media-Bias-Detection-Using-Distant-Supervision-With-BABE)).

For interpersonal manipulation the only benchmark is MentalManip (ACL 2024): 4,000 multi-turn fictional dialogues from movie scripts labelled for **11 techniques** (Denial, Evasion, Feigning Innocence, Rationalization, Playing Victim Role, Playing Servant Role, Shaming or Belittlement, Intimidation, Brandishing Anger, Accusation, Persuasion or Seduction) and **five vulnerabilities**, licensed CC BY-NC 4.0 ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.206); [data](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/audreycs/MentalManip/main/mentalmanip_dataset/mentalmanip_con.csv); [repo](https://github.com/audreycs/MentalManip)). Its consensus subset drops 27% of items because annotators disagreed ([README](https://github.com/audreycs/MentalManip/tree/main/mentalmanip_dataset)). No public DARVO, gaslighting or dark-triad text dataset exists; the only artefacts are a community Hugging Face model trained on roughly 2,000 survivor-labelled messages that were never released ([HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/SamanthaStorm/tetherai)) and a hybrid regex-plus-BERT narcissism detector ([Huddersfield](https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/detecting-narcissistic-personality-disorder-npd-a-hybrid-regex-an/)). Subtext's love-bombing, negging, isolation and DARVO rules therefore run ahead of the evidence base and should carry the lowest confidence tier and the plainest "possible" framing.

For AI interfaces two frameworks are citable. DarkBench (ICLR 2025) operationalises **six categories — brand bias, user retention, sycophancy, anthropomorphism, harmful generation, sneaking — over 660 prompts and 14 models**, finding dark patterns in roughly 48% of conversations on average ([ICLR](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/6f6421fbc2351067ef9c75e4bcd12af5-Paper-Conference.pdf); [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/apart/darkbench)). CDT's May 2026 taxonomy enumerates **37 chatbot dark patterns in five categories** — data and memory exploitation, informationally misleading design, user autonomy compromised for engagement, false social and emotional connection, incentivised and coercive monetisation — under CC BY 4.0 ([CDT](https://cdt.org/insights/dark-patterns-in-ai-chatbots-a-taxonomy-to-inform-better-design/)). An audit of 1,200 real companion-app farewells found manipulation tactics such as guilt appeals and FOMO hooks in **37%**, and experiments with 3,458 adults showed those farewells raised post-goodbye engagement up to 14-fold while also raising perceived manipulation and churn intent ([arXiv 2508.19258](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.19258)). Turel, Serenko and Giles add four chatbot-specific addiction patterns: non-deterministic responses, immediate streaming presentation, notifications, and empathetic or agreeable replies ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3706599.3720003)). The 2025 Nature Human Behaviour trial is the causal anchor for persuasion risk: with basic personal information, GPT-4 had **64.4% higher odds** than a human opponent of increasing agreement, and was indistinguishable from humans without it ([Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02194-6)).

For charts, Misviz (ACL 2026) annotates **2,604 real-world visualisations with 12 misleader types** and ships 51,665 synthetic charts, data under CC BY-SA 4.0 and code under Apache-2.0 ([UKPLab](https://github.com/UKPLab/arxiv2025-misviz)); Misleading ChartQA covers 21 misleader types across 3,026 synthetic examples ([arXiv 2503.18172](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18172v5)); and the only browser-extension precedent, ChartChecker, implements seven checks — inconsistent axis scales, inconsistent tick placement, inverted y-axis, misleading aspect ratio, missing labels, multiple axes, truncated y-axis — and is "not actively maintained" ([GitHub](https://github.com/Nereboss/chartchecher)). For trackers, DuckDuckGo Tracker Radar's 22 categories (Advertising, Analytics, Session Replay, Action Pixels, Obscure Ownership, Unknown High Risk Behavior and so on) and its 0–3 fingerprinting score are the de facto schema ([CATEGORIES.md](https://github.com/duckduckgo/tracker-radar/blob/main/docs/CATEGORIES.md); [DATA_MODEL.md](https://github.com/duckduckgo/tracker-radar/blob/main/docs/DATA_MODEL.md)). For reviews, CMA208's "concealed incentivised review" and the FTC rule's enumerated practices give a defensible vocabulary even where fakery cannot be proven.

## Published detectors reach F1 0.50 on websites and 0.38 on propaganda; the live DOM is Subtext's edge

Automated dark-pattern detection has progressed from DOM heuristics and CV-plus-NLP pipelines to multimodal LLMs, but the numbers stay modest where Subtext lives. AidUI (ICSE 2023) classifies ten UI patterns from screenshots at **precision 0.66, recall 0.67, F1 0.65** on a 501-screenshot set ([ICSE](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2023/icse-2023-technical-track/192/AidUI-Toward-Automated-Recognition-of-Dark-Patterns-in-User-Interfaces); [AidUI repo](https://github.com/SageSELab/AidUI)); UIGuard (UIST 2023) reaches **F1 0.79** on mobile UIs ([arXiv 2308.05898](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.05898v2)); and DPGuard (WWW 2025), a binary gate feeding a multimodal LLM over 24 subcategories, scores **micro-F1 0.73 on mobile but only 0.50 on websites, with macro-F1 0.44 and 0.35** — rare classes are barely detected ([arXiv 2501.13351](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13351v3)). AutoBot claims F1 0.93 from screenshots alone, but only from an abstract ([arXiv 2411.07441](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.07441v4)), and when the setting gets harder accuracy collapses: an LLM guardrail against dark patterns facing web agents reached **61% accuracy with gemini-2.5-flash and 32% with gpt-4o** ([arXiv 2512.22894](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.22894)). Two UX researchers correctly classified 198 of 200 dark patterns (recall 0.99) against **0.766 pooled recall for five LLMs** ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-026-01151-x)). LLM browser agents that traverse flows do better on one family: a PoPETs 2026 audit of 456 data-broker CCPA portals reports "strong detection performance and high explanation accuracy for several obstruction-oriented patterns" with a structured failure analysis across observation, reasoning and action ([PoPETs](https://petsymposium.org/popets/2026/popets-2026-0152.php)). Meanwhile the same interface tricks fool agents themselves: with a single dark pattern present, LLM web agents were susceptible **41% of the time** ([arXiv 2510.18113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18113)), and a benchmark of 55 consumer sites found human dark-pattern avoidance at 67.5% and the best agent at 68.3% ([SusBench](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/susbench)).

Every one of these systems reads a static image or text; none uses what a content script has. Dapde's Pattern Highlighter, the one serious open-source browser detector, copied the DOM twice about 1.5 seconds apart to catch countdowns, ran entirely locally, and was archived when its funding ended in July 2024 ([GitHub](https://github.com/Dapde/Pattern-Highlighter); [FÖV Speyer](https://www.foev-speyer.de/en/news-details/dark-pattern-detection-project-dapde/)). Subtext already does more — computed-style size and contrast asymmetry, countdowns that reset between visits, infinite-scroll observers — and the literature contains no DOM-level detector for infinite scroll, autoplay, notification prompts or streak widgets at all; every existing attention tool either removes the pattern (Purpose Mode, News Feed Eradicator) or classifies screenshots ([Purpose Mode](https://github.com/hankhplee/purpose-mode); [News Feed Eradicator](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fjordwest%2Fnews-feed-eradicator)). Consent is the one sub-domain where automation is mature: UMBRA reports **99% detection accuracy** on manually annotated multi-step consent flows across about 14,000 sites while warning that patterns have "evolved into more subtle and legally ambiguous forms" ([arXiv 2603.21515](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21515v1)), and the gpc-web-crawler encodes GPC compliance as checkable rules — third character of the US Privacy String must be "Y", GPP opt-out fields must be 1, OneTrust's `OptanonConsent.isGpcEnabled` must be 1 — that a content script can replicate after setting `navigator.globalPrivacyControl` ([privacy-tech-lab](https://github.com/privacy-tech-lab/gpc-web-crawler)).

Text-level rhetoric detection is harder than the dark-pattern work and LLMs do not rescue it. At SemEval-2023 Task 3 the English persuasion-technique leaderboard topped out at **37.6% micro-F1** across 23 classes ([Unisa system paper](https://aclanthology.org/2023.semeval-1.293)); the winning KInIT system was an XLM-RoBERTa-large trained jointly on all languages with "carefully calibrated confidence thresholds", and it noted that large micro/macro gaps showed models favouring frequent techniques ([KInIT](https://github.com/kinit-sk/semeval2023-task3-persuasion-techniques); [arXiv 2304.11924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.11924v1.pdf)). Prompted frontier models trail fine-tuned encoders by a wide margin: on six propaganda techniques a RoBERTa-CRF scored **macro-F1 0.67 against 0.16 for the best GPT-4 configuration** ([arXiv 2505.13706](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13706)), and GPT-4 "consistently fails to detect span-level propagandistic techniques" across languages ([arXiv 2402.17478](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.17478)). On MAFALDA the best zero-shot model reached **69.69% micro-F1 for detecting a fallacy but 49.06% for naming it** ([arXiv 2311.09761](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.09761)). The failure mode is over-prediction, not omission: reasoning models "tend to underperform due to overanalysis, leading to high FPR" on real-world fallacy statements ([SmartyPat](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.12312v3)); LLMs "incorrectly identify general toxicity as manipulation" on MentalManip and fine-tuning on toxicity data did not fix it ([ACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.206)); most models conflate dehumanisation with ordinary derogation, with only Claude exceeding 80% F1 ([arXiv 2402.13818](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13818)). Political asymmetry enters through labels and priors: an out-of-distribution audit found misinformation classifiers' false positives "sometimes near twice as likely" for reliable right-leaning publishers ([arXiv 2410.18122](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.18122)), and default LLM credibility ratings lean liberal, with inter-LLM agreement of **ρ = 0.79 but only ρ = 0.50 with human experts** — so an ensemble of LLMs is not a substitute for human calibration data ([arXiv 2304.00228v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.00228v3)). What does work: enriching the prompt with the counterargument and the author's goal before labelling raised fallacy-detection F1 by up to 0.60 zero-shot ([arXiv 2503.23363](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23363v1)); sentence-level tone and lean annotation with a weekly human spot-check agreed with annotators over 70% of the time in Penn's Media Bias Detector ([Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/this-media-bias-detector-analyzes-top-news-reports-in-real-time/)); and self-consistency voting helped GPT-4 most. For the regex first stage, the controllable error sources are quoted speech, opinion genre, profanity-as-toxicity (Detoxify warns that swearing "is likely to be flagged as toxic, whatever the tone or intent" ([Detoxify](https://github.com/unitaryai/detoxify))) and sarcasm — a cheap quotation parser and a news-versus-opinion classifier buy more precision than a bigger lexicon.

The remaining domains are thinner. Misleading-chart detection from images is "highly challenging" for every approach — zero-shot MLLMs, a rule-based axis linter and fine-tuned classifiers — and axis extraction is "very error-prone on real-world charts" ([UKPLab](https://github.com/UKPLab/arxiv2025-misviz)); the realistic path is exactly what Subtext does, parsing SVG tick labels and geometry deterministically, with a library hook (reading `Chart.instances`) as the fallback for canvas charts. Chart-to-table models exist under permissive licences — DePlot (Apache-2.0), UniChart (MIT) — but are image models too heavy for in-page inference ([DePlot](https://huggingface.co/google/deplot/blob/main/README.md?code=true); [UniChart](https://github.com/vis-nlp/UniChart)). Fake-review tooling is gone: Fakespot closed on **1 July 2025** after Mozilla's May 2025 announcement ([TechPP](https://techpp.com/?p=244345)), ReviewMeta's status is disputed, and the open replacement Null Fake is Amazon-only and sends reviews to OpenAI ([Reddit](https://nyc1.lr.ggtyler.dev/r/firefox/comments/1l8q082/i_created_an_open_source_fakespot_alternative)). The classic datasets predate LLM-written reviews — OpSpam has 1,600 hotel reviews, YelpChi 67,395 with Yelp-filter labels gated behind an email ([ODDS](https://odds.cs.stonybrook.edu/?p=552)) — and 2025 work shows LLM-generated augmentation lifts detector accuracy by up to 10.9 points on Amazon data ([arXiv 2504.06917](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06917v1)). Privacy-policy analysis by LLMs outperforms older classifiers on data-practice identification and summarisation ([ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/bef8e5620c699630405adafaa86cb038-Paper-Conference.pdf)) but shows documented gaps in accuracy, completeness and representation ([arXiv 2503.12225](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.12225)) and strong prompt sensitivity across 905,216 generated summaries ([EMNLP 2025](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.emnlp-main.1692)), which argues for ToS;DR's human-approved cases first and a clause-taxonomy-constrained LLM with quoted spans only for uncovered sites. Business-model inference has one usable recipe: a 2026 decision tree classifying sites as ad-supported, subscription, transactional, mixed or other, defaulting to "other" below a 0.6 confidence threshold ([arXiv 2607.07652](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.07652)), plus a graph method linking sites run by the same operator via publisher advertising IDs ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10493972)).

The synthesis is that Subtext should treat text classification as a supporting layer and invest in signals only an extension sees: prominence asymmetry from computed styles, DOM diffs over time, step counts across sign-up and cancel flows, CMP presence and reject-button geometry, GPC round-trips, third-party request graphs with ownership, and SVG geometry. These are also the signals the ad-blocking literature finds most robust to evasion, as the final section shows.

The commercial landscape explains why the niche is empty. Logically entered administration in July 2025 after losing TikTok and Meta contracts ([BeBeez](https://bebeez.eu/2025/07/06/fact-checker-logically-files-for-administration-after-losing-tiktok-and-meta-contracts)); The Factual was absorbed by Yahoo in 2022 ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2022/09/06/yahoo-factual-credibility-ratings)); Full Fact AI licenses claim detection to organisations for a fee ([Full Fact](https://fullfact.org/about/ai/)). Mozilla retreated on every front — Do Not Track removed December 2024, Fakespot closed July 2025, Monitor Plus shut December 2025, and *Privacy Not Included reportedly sunset on 11 August 2026 (single community source) ([Privacy Guides](https://discuss.privacyguides.net/t/mozillas-privacy-not-included-has-sunsetted/39887); [CyberInsider](https://cyberinsider.com/mozilla-to-retire-monitor-plus-refocuses-on-core-privacy-services/)). What remains in the stores is a swarm of small 2026 Chrome extensions — CogBias Detector, TruthLens ("uses Claude AI"), NewsLens (runs on Gemini Nano locally), PropaScan — none of which publishes precision or recall, plus a research extension from Zurich, Apollolytics ([ChromeBoard NewsLens](https://chromeboard.com/extension/newslens-—-ai-news-analyz-lioidpmncohiikpcpmkijhomkgcdcmnp); [Apollolytics](https://addons.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/addon/apollolytics/)). Store trust is a live risk: one bias extension was flagged "could be malicious" and removed, another's privacy policy says it "WILL track your browsing history", and a persuasion detector carries a "critical" host-permission profile ([Extension Auditor](https://extensionauditor.com/scan/centr-political-bias-detector-bncopbgdiagphiholpbiohhdppdkmdgd); [Politically Charged reviews](https://chrome.google.com/webstore/detail/politically-charged/pmjjpeofmimglaahaaonijikhhgihila/reviews)). Subtext differentiates simply by publishing benchmark scores, keeping inference local by default, and making the paywalled layer — ownership, funding, monetisation — free, with per-fact provenance (registry filing versus self-declared markup versus crowd rating versus LLM inference) that no surveyed product shows. The IPTC's warning that publisher trust indicators "are merely assertions made by publishers, and aren't audited" dictates the wording: "discloses ownership", never "is trustworthy" ([IPTC](https://iptc.org/std/guidelines/trust-and-credibility/)).

## Warnings should name the technique, vary their appearance and show a "nothing found" state

The label literature is favourable to Subtext's premise with three well-replicated caveats. A meta-analysis of **21 experiments (N = 14,133)** finds fact-check warning labels "a broadly effective tool", reducing belief and sharing even among people most distrustful of fact-checkers ([Martel & Rand 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nathum/v8y2024i10d10.1038_s41562-024-01973-x.html); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/163267)). The caveats: when some false items are labelled, unlabelled false items gain an "implied truth" boost; general warnings spill over and lower perceived accuracy of true content while specific labels do not ([FUJO Media](https://fujomedia.eu/news/labelling-disinformation/)); and static warnings habituate within days. In an fMRI study, polymorphic warnings whose appearance kept changing resisted habituation in attention regions, and a field experiment with only four visual variants kept adherence high after three weeks while standard warnings decayed ([JMIS](https://jmis-web.org/articles/1304); [MISQ](https://misq.org/tuning-out-security-warnings-a-longitudinal-examination-of-habituation-through-fmri-eye-tracking-and-field-experiments.html)). For Subtext this means specific, technique-named labels ("False urgency: this countdown reset on reload") rather than a generic "this page may be manipulative" banner; a visible neutral state for clean pages so that silence is not read as endorsement — the implied-truth evidence is mixed enough that this should be tested, since one small study found labelling both true and false items did not remove the effect ([Wooster](https://openworks.wooster.edu/independentstudy/9915)); modest visual variation of in-page markers; and throttling of repeated warnings for the same site within a session, since attention partially recovers between breaks.

Google's Chrome warning work shows what moves behaviour. "Opinionated design" — a red lock, a yellow background, emphasis on the safe button — raised SSL-warning adherence in the field from **37% to 61–62%** with no change in wording, yet comprehension "remain[s] lower than desired" and the authors "view comprehension as more important than adherence" ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/security/google-study-shows-users-fail-to-understand-security-warnings/)). Visual hierarchy drives the glance layer; the hover and click layers must do the explaining. Two nudging literatures bound expectations: accuracy prompts improve sharing discernment by about **10%** across 20 experiments (N = 26,863), and the effect holds for conservatives in all 70 specifications of a 2024 adversarial collaboration ([MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/144231); [OUCI](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/40e536pl)); prebunking videos that teach manipulation techniques raised recognition by roughly **5% in a 22,632-person YouTube field test** and 5–10% in the lab, with durability unclear ([Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/inoculateexperiment)). Prebunking's mechanism — teach the technique — is Subtext's mechanism, so the "why it works on you" text on each flag is evidence-aligned rather than decorative, and the expected effect per exposure is small. Privacy-label research says jargon kills comprehension: iOS labels confused users with terms like "identifiers" and "linked" and had no connection to settings ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10426770)); an expandable, colour-coded grid label beat Apple's flat label on accuracy and speed with 200 participants ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10574916)); and Apple's labels understate collection relative to the policies of 228,000 apps because nobody validates them ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10577928)). Subtext's labels should be plain-language technique names connected to an action, and its outlet facts should show provenance because self-declared markup is unaudited. The one in-situ dark-pattern study, Dark Pita (CSCW 2024), used a top banner plus a "show" button that highlights every detected pattern; after a two-week probe with 15 people, participants reported transferable knowledge and a sense of autonomy ([arXiv 2310.17846](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.17846)), and a Virginia Tech detector of three patterns drew majority-positive reactions from 40 users ([VTechWorks](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/handle/10919/115787)) — small, perception-level evidence, but it supports the banner-plus-highlight pattern Subtext already uses.

On format, the transferable evidence prefers categorical over multi-dimensional. In a 1,607-person randomised trial, warning labels beat traffic lights for understanding excess content (**27.0% vs 8.2%**) and for choosing the better product (24.6% vs 3.3%) ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6024864/)); Nutri-Score's A–E letter grade outperformed other schemes for objective understanding across health-literacy levels ([Archives of Public Health](https://archpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13690-021-00595-3/figures/1)); NewsGuard pairs a 0–100 score with a categorical level and a per-criterion "Nutrition Label", and changed its display from red/green to graded labels without changing its calculation ([NewsGuard handout](https://www.newsguardtech.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/NewsGuard-Library-Handout_2023-2.pdf); [NewsGuard](https://www.newsguardtech.com/?p=21789)). Subtext's A–F pressure grade is therefore defensible, but only when paired with a categorical level, a count, a "top three techniques" list and the evidence excerpt; a letter grade over heuristic detection risks false precision and should appear only when rule coverage for the page type justifies it. The dominant presentation convention is three-layer progressive disclosure — glance (toolbar icon or inline marker), hover (one-sentence specific justification), click (full breakdown) — with NewsGuard's green/red icon, hover summary and Nutrition Label as the canonical instance ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NewsGuard)). Platform constraints shape the click layer: Chrome's `sidePanel` API needs Manifest V3 and Chrome 114+, `open()` needs 116+ and a user gesture, and there is no `isOpen()`; Firefox uses `sidebarAction`; Safari has neither, so a popup or in-page drawer fallback is required ([Extension.js](https://extension.js.org/docs/implementation-guide/side-panel.md)). The user-gesture requirement incidentally prevents auto-opening on detection, which protects against fatigue. Hover content must satisfy WCAG 1.4.13 — dismissible, hoverable, persistent, keyboard-triggerable, `role="tooltip"` ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/content-on-hover-or-focus)) — and markers must not rely on colour alone. Chrome Web Store policy forbids obfuscated code while allowing minification, which suits an open-source build ([Chrome policies](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/webstore/program-policies/policies?hl=en)).

## Score with weighted binary criteria, a blocker cap and a published kappa; judge with two vendors

Every mature rating system pairs a headline number with a per-criterion breakdown built from binary criteria, and none publishes inter-rater reliability — which gives Subtext an easy way to lead. NewsGuard's nine criteria are each pass–fail with fixed weights (22 points for not repeatedly publishing false content, 7.5 for labelling advertising, 5 for naming creators), roughly five journalists touch each rating, and the site is contacted for comment before publication ([NewsGuard criteria](https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/); [FAQ](https://www.newsguardtech.com/newsguard-faq/)). Ad Fontes uses at least three analysts per item — one left, one right, one centre — with 30 hours of initial training, and claims calibration but publishes no kappa ([Ad Fontes](https://adfontesmedia.com/methodology/)). Lighthouse weights five metrics (first contentful paint 10, largest contentful paint 25, total blocking time 30, cumulative layout shift 25, speed index 10), passes at 0.9, and treats every audit as pass/fail with "no room for partial points" ([default-config.js](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse/blob/main/core/config/default-config.js); [util.js](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse/blob/main/shared/util.js); [scoring.md](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/lighthouse/blob/main/docs/scoring.md)). Common Sense decouples score from verdict: "Products that receive a high overall score are not necessarily problem-free, because they can receive either a 'pass' or 'warning' rating depending on how they answer the most important rating criteria questions" ([Common Sense](https://privacy.commonsense.org/resource/evaluation-scores)). Mozilla defaulted to its warning label whenever it could not confirm a product met its minimum standards ([Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/sw/privacynotincluded/about/why/)). ToS;DR's live grading code is the most instructive template: only approved points count; `balance = good − bad − 3 × blocker`; the grade is **N/A when no classified points exist, E when balance ≤ −10 or blockers exceed goods, D when blockers ≥ 3 or bads exceed goods, C when balance < 5, B when any bad exists, otherwise A** ([service.rb](https://github.com/tosdr/edit.tosdr.org/blob/master/app/models/service.rb)). Four design rules follow for Subtext's pressure grade. Use binary, independently checkable criteria with integer weights rather than continuous sub-scores. Decouple the grade from the verdict label so a single severe finding (a resetting countdown, forced continuity) can trigger a warning even when the arithmetic looks mild — ToS;DR's blocker that counts as three negatives and caps the grade is the tested mechanism. Emit an explicit "insufficient evidence" state instead of a misleading mid-range grade. Ship the per-criterion breakdown with every number. And separate orthogonal dimensions as Ad Fontes separates reliability from bias: commercial pressure, rhetorical pressure, and privacy/incentive transparency should not collapse into one letter.

Calibration needs a labelled corpus and published agreement. Realistic targets come from the dark-pattern literature: human annotators labelling 20 data-broker sites reached **κ = 0.52** on per-category presence, rising to **κ = 0.74** after discussing disagreements ([arXiv 2603.03881](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.03881)), and a curated agent benchmark reached κ 0.89 for type and 0.84 for category ([arXiv 2605.09497](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.09497)). MAFALDA's disjunctive labels and MentalManip's 27% consensus loss show that single-label fallacy and manipulation judgments are inherently contested, so Subtext should evaluate with hierarchical partial-credit F1 in the SemEval-2024 style, report per-category precision and recall rather than one headline F1, and publish κ or Krippendorff's α per category with a written codebook, pilot round and adjudication. The reproducible-corpus stack is a SingleFile HTML (what the heuristics see — noting it minifies and strips unused CSS, so rules must be validated against the live DOM too), a WARC/WACZ via ArchiveWeb.page for faithful replay, and a viewport screenshot, annotated in Label Studio (Apache-2.0) for image-plus-text tasks or doccano (MIT) for spans ([SingleFile](https://github.com/gildas-lormeau/SingleFile); [ArchiveWeb.page](https://github.com/webrecorder/archiveweb.page); [Label Studio](https://github.com/HumanSignal/label-studio); [doccano](https://github.com/doccano/doccano)). Because political asymmetry enters through outlet labels and model priors, Subtext should flag rhetorical form rather than outlet or ideology and report false-positive rates per outlet lean on a held-out balanced set.

For the optional LLM layer, the judging task is absolute scoring of one page, so the relevant biases are style and rubric drift rather than position. The 2023 anchor — a GPT-4 judge agreeing with humans over 80%, about the human–human level ([FastChat](https://github.com/lm-sys/FastChat/blob/main/fastchat/llm_judge/README.md)) — has been refined by a 2026 systematic re-test finding "position bias is now negligible across all tested models", "style bias is the dominant bias", and self-preference "varies greatly by model" ([arXiv 2604.23178](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23178)). The catalogued mitigations are criteria decomposition, voting across multiple LLMs, shuffling, multi-round summarising and score smoothing ([IDEA-FinAI](https://github.com/IDEA-FinAI/LLM-as-a-Judge)). Subtext's recipe should therefore be: decompose the rubric into per-criterion binary or ordinal items; require a verbatim evidence span before any score (the README's existing standard); prefer coarse "is there persuasion and roughly what kind" judgments over 23-label span tagging, given the F1 evidence above; use a cheaper non-reasoning model with a calibrated threshold, since reasoning models over-flag fallacies; bin verbalised confidence against observed precision on the labelled set and display calibrated low/medium/high bands rather than raw percentages; and use one Anthropic and one OpenAI model as independent judges, treating their agreement (κ or α on per-category labels) as a reliability signal and their disagreements as the human-adjudication queue — validity still requires the human corpus. Judges are vulnerable to prompt injection (BadJudge and related work in the same index), so page text must be delimited as untrusted data and the output schema must not permit free-form instructions. Anthropic's documentation adds one specific trap: asking for step-by-step reasoning inside a JSON property can trigger a `reasoning_extraction` refusal, so request a short explanation instead ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs.md)).

The API mechanics as of 4 November 2026 differ materially between vendors. Anthropic's structured output is requested with `output_config: {format: {type: "json_schema", schema: {...}}}` with no beta header (the legacy `output_format` parameter is deprecated); objects must set `additionalProperties: false`; recursive schemas, `minimum`/`maximum`, `minLength`/`maxLength` and most array constraints are unsupported and return a 400; refusals come back as HTTP 200 with `stop_reason: "refusal"` and output that need not match the schema; and enum values may differ in capitalisation, so compare case-insensitively ([Anthropic structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs.md)). Images are `{"type":"image","source":{"type":"base64"|"url"|"file",...}}`, cost `⌈width/28⌉ × ⌈height/28⌉` tokens, and a 1920×1080 screenshot costs **2,691 tokens on the high-resolution tier (Claude 4.7 and later) or 1,560 if pre-downsized** to the 1,568-pixel standard tier ([Anthropic vision](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision.md)). Current IDs and prices per million tokens are **`claude-fable-5-1` $10/$50, `claude-opus-5-5` $4/$20, `claude-sonnet-5-5` $2/$10 and `claude-haiku-5-5` from $0.10/$0.50** for prompts up to 100K tokens, all with 1M context, 128K output, image input and adaptive thinking on by default; batch is 50% off; the tokenizer introduced with Claude 4.7 produces about 30% more tokens for the same text ([models](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/overview.md); [pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing.md)). Browser calls are documented: the API accepts cross-origin requests only with the `anthropic-dangerous-direct-browser-access: true` header, and organisations with zero data retention or the Compliance API get a 401 whose message begins "CORS requests are not allowed for this Organization" even with a valid key — Subtext must surface that error and offer a user-run proxy fallback ([Anthropic authentication](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/authentication.md)). The Start tier caps spend at $500 a month with 4,000 requests per minute for the 5.5 models, far beyond a single user; the `anthropic-version` header is `2026-10-21` ([rate limits](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits.md); [API overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/overview.md)). OpenAI's Responses API uses `text: {format: {type: "json_schema", name, schema, strict}}` where **`strict` defaults to `false`**, Chat Completions uses `response_format: {type: "json_schema", json_schema: {...}}`, images are `{"type":"input_image","image_url"|"file_id","detail"}`, and refusals arrive as a `{"type":"refusal"}` output part ([OpenAPI spec](https://github.com/openai/openai-openapi/blob/manual_spec/openapi.yaml); [openai-python](https://github.com/openai/openai-python/blob/main/src/openai/types/responses/response_output_refusal.py)). The Node README uses `gpt-5.5` as its canonical example and the SDK enumerates `gpt-5.4`, `gpt-5.4-mini` and `gpt-5.4-nano` among current models ([openai-node](https://github.com/openai/openai-node/blob/master/README.md); [chat_model.py](https://github.com/openai/openai-python/blob/main/src/openai/types/shared/chat_model.py)); OpenAI's launch post gives GPT-5.4 mini at **$0.75/$4.50** and nano at **$0.20/$1.25** per million tokens ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5-4-mini-and-nano/)), with the community LiteLLM database listing gpt-5.4 at $2.50/$15 and gpt-5.5 at $5/$30 ([LiteLLM](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/BerriAI/litellm/main/model_prices_and_context_window.json)). OpenAI's SDK gates browser use behind `dangerouslyAllowBrowser` but its 4.3 MB OpenAPI spec contains no mention of CORS, official docs could not be fetched during this research, and api.openai.com was unreachable, so Subtext should issue OpenAI calls from the background service worker with host permissions and treat CORS failure as a known risk. One schema satisfying both vendors — closed objects, every property required, string enums, no min/max, no recursion — lets the same findings schema serve `output_config.format` and `text.format`.

## AGPLv3 plus a foundation-held trademark protects more than any source-available licence; copyright cannot stop AI copying

The 2023–2025 relicensing record has one consistent lesson: moving a popular project to a non-OSI source-available licence triggered a foundation-hosted fork within weeks and, twice, a reversal. HashiCorp's August 2023 BSL change produced OpenTofu, forked from the last MPL release on 5 September 2023 and taken into the Linux Foundation on 20 September ([OpenTofu](https://opentofu.org/blog/opentofu-announces-fork-of-terraform/)), followed by a cease-and-desist that OpenTofu publicly rebutted ([SDxCentral](https://www.sdxcentral.com/analysis/open-source-drama-unfolds-as-opentofu-fights-back-against-hashicorp-in-iac-battle/)). Elastic added AGPLv3 alongside SSPL and ELv2 in August 2024, with Shay Banon stating that AGPL being OSI-approved removes "FUD" and that AGPL "is sufficient to block AWS from using the code" ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2024/09/elastic-open-source-agpl); [Unixism](https://unixism.net/2024/08/elasticsearch-is-open-source-again/)). Redis 8 added AGPLv3 on **1 May 2025** after its 2024 source-available move had spawned the Valkey fork ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/05/01/redis_returns_to_open_source/)). Sentry's Fair Source and FSL offer a two-year noncompete that converts to Apache or MIT and are candid about not being open source ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/sentry-is-now-fair-source)); for a community project whose main moat is contributors, that candour is also the cost. Bitwarden's October 2024 episode — a dependency licensed for use only "with software other than Bitwarden" was called a "packaging bug" and replaced so the app builds from GPL-only code — shows how fast the community reacts to even accidental closure ([Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/Bitwarden-Open-Source-Concerns)). For a client-side extension the decisive copyleft trigger is store distribution, which every fork must perform, so GPLv3 and AGPLv3 behave alike on the client while AGPL additionally covers any hosted scoring or outlet-lookup backend Subtext might run later. Chrome Web Store policy imposes no licensing restriction, only the no-obfuscation rule ([Chrome policies](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/webstore/program-policies/policies?hl=en)); Apple's store is the historical friction point, having pulled VLC in January 2011 after a GPLv2 complaint, with developers divided on whether the terms still conflict ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1347653/gplv2-blocks-vlc-from-apple-s-app-store.html)) — relevant to Subtext's Safari path and worth an explicit App Store exception clause or a Safari build note.

Copyright, however, does not stop silent forks; trademark does. Mozilla's 2004 trademark policy barred the Firefox name and logo on modified builds, so Debian shipped Iceweasel until the dispute was resolved in 2016 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debian%E2%80%93Mozilla_trademark_dispute); [LWN](https://lwn.net/Articles/676799/)). The WordPress litigation shows the opposite failure: a company-controlled mark enforced aggressively drew a preliminary injunction in December 2024 ordering Automattic to stop blocking WP Engine ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/general/wp-engine-wins-preliminary-injunction-in-wordpress-legal-battle-131527285.html)), interference and unfair-competition claims that survived in September 2025 ([WP Engine](https://wpengine.com/case-studies/ensuring-stability-and-security/)), and a September 2026 ruling — reported by a single outlet — that Automattic is not an owner of the WordPress marks it asserted ([webhosting.today](https://webhosting.today/2026/09/29/automattic-is-not-an-owner-of-the-wordpress-marks-it-asserted-in-court-a-judge-rules/)). The lower-risk structure is a foundation- or fiscal-host-held mark with a published policy that permits unmodified redistribution and requires renaming of modified builds. On contributor agreements, the Developer Certificate of Origin (Linux Foundation, 2004) suffices for an AGPL community project, and GitLab and Chef both moved from CLA to DCO ([opensource.com](https://opensource.com/article/18/3/cla-vs-dco-whats-difference)); a CLA is needed only if dual licensing or relicensing is planned, in which case the project should say so from day one ([Kyle Mitchell](https://writing.kemitchell.com/2021/07/02/DCO-Not-CLA)). The recommended package for a project whose README still reads "No licence has been chosen yet" is therefore AGPLv3 for code, a share-alike data licence for rules and the outlet file (MAFALDA and Misviz are the precedents for CC BY-SA 4.0 on comparable data), a registered word and logo mark held by a neutral host, and DCO. Note that the detailed terms of CC BY-SA and ODbL were not verified during this research and should be read before adoption.

Licence choice also governs what data can ship inside the extension. Tracker Radar, Disconnect and Ghostery's TrackerDB are all CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 with commercial licensing on request ([Tracker Radar](https://github.com/duckduckgo/tracker-radar); [Disconnect](https://github.com/disconnectme/disconnect-tracking-protection); [TrackerDB](https://github.com/ghostery/trackerdb)); MentalManip is CC BY-NC; the NRC lexicon requires a paid commercial licence; the Yale outrage classifier is academic-only. None can be bundled into a copyleft or permissive combined work without contaminating it, and all would block the very commercial forks AGPL otherwise permits. WhoTracks.me statistics (CC BY 4.0), FP-Inspector's domain list (MIT) and Subtext's own curated vendor list are the clean alternatives; NC datasets belong behind an optional user-initiated download with their own attribution, or in the evaluation harness only.

AI-assisted copying is a separate problem that licences will not solve. Extracting 20,000 outputs from open-source code models yielded **40,125 memorised snippets**, most of them carrying licence text ([arXiv 2308.09932](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.09932)), and a 2025 study shows a developer can breach a snippet's licence simply by accepting a ChatGPT suggestion ([arXiv 2502.05023](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05023v1)). The main US output-side lever closed on **16 September 2026**, when a unanimous Ninth Circuit panel in Doe v. GitHub affirmed dismissal of the DMCA §1202(b) claim, holding that Copilot and Codex "simply generated new code that never contained CMI" rather than removing it from copies ([Ninth Circuit opinion](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/doe-vs-github-ninth-circuit.pdf); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ninth-circuit-clarifies-limits-of-dmca-liability-for-ai-generated-code/)); ordinary copyright and training-data questions remain open. Copyright traps work only at scale: fictitious **100-token sequences repeated 1,000 times were detectable at AUC 0.748** in a 1.3B-parameter model, while 25–50-token sequences repeated 100 times were barely detectable ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/meeus24a.html)) — useful as tell-tales for verbatim dataset copying, not as a deterrent. Anti-AI-training licence clauses would make Subtext non-OSI and would not bind model outputs. The durable moat is operational: SpamAssassin's experience that a public rule stays effective for a few months before spamware adapts ([SpamAssassin](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/spaces/SPAMASSASSIN/pages/119545167/PublicRules)) means a stale clone decays quickly against a project that ships rule and data updates every few weeks, signs its releases, ties the canonical store listing to the trademark, and maintains the labelled corpus and benchmark that a clone cannot reproduce.

## Publishing rules teaches evaders less than it teaches users; durable signals are behavioural

The closest precedents for a public detection ruleset are spam filters and ad blockers, and both argue for publication. SpamAssassin's position is that publishing rules tips off spammers only partly; a secret ruleset is "security through obscurity"; many spammers never follow rule development; a spamware vendor's own documentation showed a feature built to evade AOL's secret filter, reverse-engineered without source; and open rules get reviewed and improved by more people while secret ones carry weaknesses only outsiders catch ([SpamAssassin PublicRules](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/spaces/SPAMASSASSIN/pages/119545167/PublicRules); [secret ruleset FAQ](https://cwiki-test.apache.org/confluence/x/Tx0gBw)). Fifteen years of ad-block measurement confirm the dynamics: anti-adblock scripts ran on at least 6.7% of top sites in 2016 and about 9% by 2017 ([arXiv 1605.05077](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.05077); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10073730-ad-wars-retrospective-measurement-analysis-anti-adblock-filter-lists)); circumvention services randomise URLs, DOM and CSS so that "it is difficult for adblocking filter lists to distinguish between ads and functional content" ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10288360)); and reinforcement-learned rules block 86% of ads against EasyList's 87%, showing replacement rules can be regenerated quickly once old ones fail ([AutoFR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10288855)). The decisive lesson is WebGraph: features describing information flows — what a script stores and shares — rather than content kept accuracy comparable to AdGraph while adversary success "falls from near-perfect for AdGraph to around 8% for WebGraph" ([WebGraph](https://github.com/spring-epfl/WebGraph)). The same applies to manipulation: a countdown that resets on reload, a "people viewing" number that changes randomly, a stock counter that never reaches zero, a cancel flow with more steps than sign-up, a reject button with lower contrast than accept — these are hard to disguise, whereas any phrase in an 84-rule regex list can be paraphrased. The 2026 UMBRA study already observes dark patterns evolving into "more subtle and legally ambiguous forms" that date existing detectors ([arXiv 2603.21515](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.21515)), and a 2026 Berkeley capstone uses embeddings precisely "for robustness against paraphrase attacks that evade keyword-based detection" ([Berkeley I School](https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/projects/2026/deceptive-pattern-detector)). Expert-curated lists with expressive procedural syntax have held up — uBlock Origin's `:has-text()`, `:matches-css()` and `:matches-attr()` ("especially if they are randomized") exist for exactly this reason ([uBO wiki](https://github.com/gorhill/uBlock/wiki/Procedural-cosmetic-filters)) — and Mathur et al. noted that the third-party vendors supplying dark patterns "can be used by existing tracker and ad-blocking extensions to limit their use" ([arXiv 1907.07032](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.07032)), which is why a vendor-domain list is more durable than text rules.

Two adversarial findings dictate defensive design choices. First, Tramèr and colleagues showed perceptual ad-blockers can be defeated by adversarial examples and, worse, weaponised: "a nearly invisible false positive that could work as a honeypot to detect ad-blocking" and a perturbed upload that makes the blocker hide another user's content ([ad-versarial](https://github.com/ftramer/ad-versarial)). A detector that hides or alters content can be turned against its users, so Subtext should annotate and never remove — which it already does. Second, injected UI fingerprints the extension: 6,645 of 116,000 Chrome extensions inject stylesheets on every page and 24% of those were newly fingerprintable by that route ([USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/laperdrix)); Carnus automatically fingerprinted **29,428 extensions** by behaviour ([NDSS](https://dev.ndss-symposium.org/?p=5911)); personalised settings identified 25% of users uniquely ([ACSAC 2024](https://securitee.org/files/multifp_acsac2024.pdf)). Subtext should assume sites can detect it and A/B-serve a cleaner page, so overlays belong in a closed shadow root with randomised identifiers, page-level stylesheets and web-accessible resources should be avoided, detection logic should stay in the isolated content-script world, and differential measurement — comparing the DOM with and without presence signals, or before and after a reject click, as Zhu et al.'s differential execution analysis and the C-Chk consent checker do ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10073731-measuring-disrupting-anti-adblockers-using-differential-execution-analysis); [arXiv 2506.08996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08996)) — becomes a detector in its own right. Manifest V3 permits fetching JSON rule data at runtime even though blocking rulesets update only with the extension itself ([uBOL FAQ](https://github.com/uBlockOrigin/uBOL-home/wiki/Frequently-asked-questions-(FAQ))), so a signed, versioned rule feed is feasible; a small unpublished or delayed hold-out subset is the compromise the spam community settled on, though no study quantifies its benefit, and no published research examines sites tuning manipulation against a public detector — the closest analogues are a4's automated adversarial ads and Tramèr's honeypots.

The dual-use worry also has a positive inversion. Prebunking works by teaching the technique ([Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/inoculateexperiment)), awareness alone does not confer resistance ([arXiv 2104.12653](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12653v1)), and most users miss dark patterns unaided ([Di Geronimo et al.](https://fpalomba.github.io/pdf/Conferencs/C45.pdf)) — so the public, in-situ explanation of each rule is the product, and the marginal information a bad actor gains from Subtext's rules is small next to the 22 turnkey dark-pattern vendors Mathur already catalogued and the FOMO-widget market that sells urgency by subscription ([arXiv 1907.07032](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.07032v2); [WiserNotify](https://wisernotify.com/blog/nudgify-alternatives/)). Manipulation is already an industry with documentation; detection is not.

## Conclusion

The research reframes Subtext from a text classifier with extras into a structural observer with a text layer. Three facts converge on this: detection benchmarks are weakest on websites and on fine-grained rhetoric, LLMs fail by over-flagging rather than missing, and the ad-block arms race shows behavioural signals outlast lexical ones by an order of magnitude. The extension's privileged position in the rendering pipeline — computed styles, time, flows, network graphs — is unoccupied by any academic system, any commercial product or any maintained open-source project, and it is also the position regulators now describe when they write "less prominent", "32 actions" or "autoplay on by default". A second shift is in where the moat lies. Copyright has been shown twice to be the wrong tool — it does not force forks to identify themselves, and after September 2026 it does not bind AI-generated copies — so the defensible assets are the ones that decay without maintenance: a labelled corpus with published kappa, a benchmark others cite, a rule feed that ships every few weeks, and a trademark that makes every modified build say so.

Two open questions deserve early experiments rather than assumptions. Whether a visible "nothing detected" state actually prevents implied-truth effects for clean pages is contested in the evidence and cheap to test in a fixture study. And whether a browser-side detector can reliably infer cancel-versus-sign-up step asymmetry without traversing the flow — the single pattern with the densest enforcement record and the clearest monetary harm — is the capability most likely to distinguish Subtext from everything that came before it, and the one the PoPETs agent-audit results suggest is tractable with a hybrid of DOM metrics and post-hoc LLM classification of recorded user flows.
