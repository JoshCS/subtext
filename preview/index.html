Subtext is a browser extension that helps you see who’s behind what you’re reading, what they want you to do, and the incentives behind their content.

What to look for on a page

On the museum page I want to know who paid for the research. If I’m shopping for shoes, what dark patterns are being used to trick me into a purchase?

Who stands to gain?

A list of the top five running shoes doesn’t tell you whether the publisher earns the highest commission on those five shoes. But we can flag the affiliate links they’re using.

Why am I being rushed?

A timer tells you there are only ten minutes left on the sale, but every time you refresh the page, the timer starts over.

What am I agreeing to?

“Cancel anytime” can still mean you’ve paid for the year. If you quit in February, can you get the rest back?

Where did this story come from?

Say you’re reading a story about reducing taxes on billionaires. It was originally written by a news organization owned by a billionaire who has some level of editorial control. That matters.

Go further with Investigate

Investigate uses your AI of choice to dig deeper into who’s behind what you’re seeing, what they want you to do, how they’re trying to get you to do it, and what their incentives might be. Connect your Claude or ChatGPT API key and give it a page you’re curious about. When something turns up you can see the exact passages it read.

Suppose you’re changing careers.

Say there’s a free guide to getting a job in a new field. And it’s a good guide, there’s plenty in there that’ll help you. Then you get to the part where you need this particular certificate. Off you go to click purchase on a link to a course.

Turns out the author runs the training company. Maybe it’s a perfectly good course. But if I’m about to buy it, that’s a fairly important thing to know about the advice I’ve just been given.

Why build this now?

When I look at a page, I usually start with a pretty basic question: who is the publisher of this, and what are their incentives and funding? Is the content I’m looking at content that overlaps with those incentives and funding in any meaningful way? If they’re funded by a billionaire and the article is about not taxing billionaires, that matters a lot. If the same publisher has a story about making a good weeknight pasta with lemon and peas, well, knowing who owns the paper probably isn’t the most interesting thing you could tell me about dinner.

With AI, there’s been a massive increase in the volume of content being shared. It’s getting harder to trust what we’re seeing and reading online. If a page is clearly designed to point me to go and click on a link to a course, then that’s useful info. What I want to do with Subtext is give you that insider industry perspective that somebody might not otherwise have.

To give you the bit of information you need to make up your own mind about it.

Install Subtext

Load the zip in Chrome. You can do the standard checks without an AI key.

Download v0.2.0 (.zip)
  1. Unzip the downloaded file.
  2. Open chrome://extensions and turn on Developer mode.
  3. Select Load unpacked and choose the unzipped extension folder.
  4. Open a page and click Subtext in the Extensions menu.

Before you install

If I open Subtext on a shop, who hears about it?

The checks run in your browser. Subtext may fetch site policies, ad records and seller details. There’s no Subtext server collecting scans. Exclude sites in Settings.

Show context sends lookup terms to outside sources. Google Trends uses your Google cookies, so Google can tie the topic to your account. You can turn that off.

If I try Investigate once, what will it cost?

About 11¢ on average with the default Claude model across 43 test pages. Web search can add roughly 5¢. Your provider bills your key for actual usage.

Click Investigate and your provider gets page excerpts, headings, controls and source material. That may include personal details printed on the page.

What if Subtext misinterprets something?

Subtext shows you the methods it uses and the assumptions it’s making. When it uses AI, it does its best to cite sources and explain the thinking behind its insights. But AI can misread a quote, overlook a connection or miss things altogether. It can’t truly interpret intent. Those are judgment calls and shouldn’t be taken as fact.
